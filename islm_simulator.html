What survives the interest rate

Fiscal policy shifts IS by the full multiplier. Then the money market answers back. IS-LM exists to show you how much of the push is left when it does.

Ten questions. Most turn on the slopes: how much of a policy works depends on how steep the other curve is.

Two markets, one interest rate

IS is every combination of output and interest rate at which the goods market clears. LM is every combination at which the money market clears. Only where they cross are both true at once, and the interest rate is what forces them to agree.

CurveWhy it slopes that wayWhat shifts it
IS, downward A lower interest rate makes more investment projects worth doing. Higher investment raises demand, and the multiplier turns that into a larger rise in output. Government spending, taxes, consumer confidence, business confidence, net exports. Anything that changes planned spending at a given interest rate.
LM, upward Higher output means more transactions and more demand for money. With the real money supply fixed, the interest rate must rise to persuade people to hold the same quantity. The nominal money supply, the price level (which sets real balances), and shifts in liquidity preference.

Shifters of IS

  • Government spendingShifts IS right by the full multiplier times the change.
  • TaxesShifts IS left, but by less than an equal spending cut, because part of a tax rise comes out of saving.
  • Consumer confidenceAutonomous consumption at any level of income.
  • Business confidenceInvestment demand at any interest rate. This is the animal-spirits term.
  • Net exportsForeign incomes and the exchange rate.

Shifters of LM

  • Nominal money supplyOpen market operations. More money shifts LM right and down.
  • The price levelWhat matters is real balances, so a higher price level shrinks the real money supply and shifts LM left. This is the bridge from IS-LM to the AD curve.
  • Liquidity preferenceA flight to cash raises money demand at every interest rate and shifts LM left, which is what a financial panic looks like here.

The two slopes decide everything

  • Flat LMInterest rates barely move, so fiscal policy works almost fully and monetary policy does very little. The liquidity trap.
  • Steep LMInterest rates move sharply, so fiscal policy is largely crowded out and monetary policy is powerful. The classical case.
  • Steep ISInvestment ignores interest rates, so a rising rate destroys little of a fiscal push.
  • Flat ISInvestment is highly rate-sensitive, so crowding out is severe and monetary policy has a strong grip.

Five things to demonstrate live

Crowding out, measured

Run the fiscal preset. The hollow marker shows where output would land if the interest rate never moved, the solid dot shows where it actually lands, and the shaded strip between them is what the money market took back. Watch investment in the composition bar fall as it happens.

The same policy, two economies

Apply an identical spending increase with LM flat and then with LM steep. The realised multiplier collapses from close to its textbook value to almost nothing. The policy never changed; the money market's response did.

Why the trap matters

In the trap preset, printing money barely moves output because the interest rate has nowhere to fall, while fiscal policy runs at nearly full strength. That is the whole argument for spending your way out of a deep slump rather than easing your way out.

The policy mix

Combine a spending increase with monetary accommodation. Output rises with the interest rate held near where it started, so investment is not squeezed. Same output, very different composition, which is the point fiscal and monetary coordination is usually making.

The bridge to AD

Move the price level slider and watch output fall as real balances shrink. Trace that relationship between the price level and output and you have drawn the AD curve of the AS-AD model. The two diagrams are the same economy seen from different angles.
