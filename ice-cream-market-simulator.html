Price and non-price factors, in one page

Everything in the demand and supply model does one of two jobs. Own price moves you along a fixed curve. Every other determinant moves the whole curve to a new position.

  Price factor — the good's own price Non-price factors — everything else
What it does Moves you along the existing curve. Shifts the entire curve left or right.
The right words A change in quantity demanded, or quantity supplied. A change in demand, or supply.
On the diagram The curve stays put; the point slides. A new curve is drawn; a new equilibrium is found.
In the simulator Step 4, the controlled price. Steps 2 and 3, the shifter sliders.

Seven shifters of demand

  • IncomeThe direction depends entirely on the type of good, which is the point of Step 1.
  • Price of substitutesFrozen yoghurt gets dearer, buyers switch across, ice cream demand moves right.
  • Price of complementsCones and toppings get dearer, the package costs more, demand moves left.
  • Tastes and preferencesA viral flavour, a sugar scare, a festival. Advertising lives here too.
  • Number of buyersPopulation, tourist season, a new university campus down the road.
  • ExpectationsIf buyers expect a price rise next month, they buy today and demand moves right now.
  • Season and weatherThe classic ice cream shifter. Thirty-eight degrees does more than any advertisement.

Six shifters of supply

  • Input pricesMilk, sugar, cream, cold-chain electricity, wages. Costs up, supply left.
  • TechnologyBetter freezers and faster batch churns lower unit cost and push supply right.
  • Taxes and subsidiesA per-unit tax acts like a cost and moves supply left; a subsidy moves it right.
  • Number of sellersNew entrants add capacity at every price.
  • Producer expectationsIf sellers expect a higher price next month, they hold stock back and supply falls now.
  • Price of goods related in productionIf gelato earns more on the same plant, ice cream supply gives up capacity.

Why the three goods behave differently

A price change always splits into two effects. The substitution effect says a dearer good gets swapped out. The income effect says a dearer good makes you poorer in real terms, and what you do with that depends on the good.

Normal good

Both effects push the same way. Demand slopes down, and a rise in income shifts the whole curve right. Premium ice cream in a growing economy.

Inferior good

The substitution effect still wins, so demand still slopes down. But a rise in income shifts the curve left as buyers trade up to something better. Cheap tub ice cream when salaries rise.

Giffen good

A rare inferior good where the income effect is so strong it overturns the substitution effect, and the demand curve slopes up. It needs a large share of a poor household's budget and no close substitute, which is why the credible evidence comes from staples such as rice and wheat, not from dessert.

Watch one result carefully in Giffen mode: an increase in supply lowers the price and lowers the quantity traded, because the upward-sloping demand curve is followed downwards. That is the diagnostic students are usually asked to explain.
