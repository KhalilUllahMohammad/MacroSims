Ten questions on the model. Most turn on where the economy sits relative to potential, and on which horizon you are being asked about.

Why SRAS carries on past LRAS

Potential output is not a physical ceiling. It is the output an economy can sustain without pressure building, and in the short run it can be exceeded. Overtime is worked, shifts are added, plants run through maintenance windows, workers are hired from an ever shorter queue. All of that is possible, none of it is free, and none of it lasts.

Stretch of SRASWhat is happeningHow a demand shock splits
Flat, below potential Idle machines and unemployed workers. Extra orders are met at close to the posted price because unit costs barely move. Almost entirely into output. The multiplier runs near full strength and inflation hardly stirs.
Crossing potential The slack is used up. Firms start competing for the same workers and the same inputs. Split between output and prices, with the balance tilting towards prices as the economy tightens.
Steep, above potential Overheating. Overtime premiums, bottlenecks, delivery delays and bidding wars for labour. Mostly into prices. Output does rise above potential, but each extra point costs far more inflation than the last.
LRAS Every contract renegotiated. Nothing nominal has any grip on real output. Entirely into prices. Output sits at potential regardless of where demand is.

The overheating stretch is what makes the short run and the long run genuinely different claims rather than two labels for one diagram. In the short run output above potential is a real, observable state. In the long run it is impossible, and the model shows you the mechanism that removes it: rising costs pull SRAS up until the boom has been converted entirely into a higher price level.

Shifters of AD

  • ConsumptionConfidence, household wealth, credit conditions.
  • InvestmentInterest rates, business expectations, capacity utilisation.
  • Government spendingPowerful through the slack, inflationary past potential.
  • TaxesHigher taxes cut disposable income and shift AD left.
  • Net exportsForeign incomes and the exchange rate.
  • Monetary policyEasier money shifts AD right, with a regime-dependent result.

Shifters of SRAS

  • Nominal wagesThe biggest cost, and the one written into contracts.
  • Energy and imported inputsLifts the whole curve: stagflation.
  • Expected price levelWhat firms assume when they set prices for the year.
  • Short-run productivityLower unit costs without touching potential output.

Shifters of LRAS

  • Labour forceDemography, participation, migration.
  • Capital stockAccumulated investment.
  • TechnologyA permanent gain moves SRAS and LRAS together.
  • Institutions and skillsRegulation, education, job matching.

Five things to demonstrate live

The same policy, two answers

Apply an identical stimulus from a slump and again from full employment. Through the slack it buys output cheaply; past potential it buys a small amount of output and a lot of inflation. Nothing changed but the starting point.

Overheating is real, and temporary

Run the boom preset. Output stands above potential and unemployment below its natural rate, which is a genuine short-run state, not an error. Then advance the periods and watch costs climb until the entire gain has turned into price level.

The Keynesian limiting case

Drag stickiness to its minimum and the flat stretch becomes genuinely horizontal. A demand shock through the slack is then pure output with no inflation whatsoever, which is the extreme case the textbook draws as a flat line.

Why the gap can persist

Leave rigidity on, open a recessionary gap and run to the long run. Nothing moves. Booms unwind on their own because costs rise easily; slumps do not, because costs fall reluctantly. That asymmetry is the case for active demand policy.

Supply shocks still bite

Raise energy costs and the whole curve lifts. The economy slides back up AD to less output at a higher price level, and no demand policy can undo both halves of that at once.
