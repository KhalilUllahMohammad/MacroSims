Student guide

What the model is for

The Mundell–Fleming model is the IS–LM model rebuilt for a small economy that trades goods and financial assets with the rest of the world. Robert Mundell and J. Marcus Fleming developed it in the early 1960s, and Mundell's work on it was part of his 1999 Nobel Prize.

Its central lesson still drives policy debates: whether fiscal or monetary policy can move output depends on the exchange-rate regime. Under a floating exchange rate, monetary policy moves output and fiscal policy does not. Under a fixed exchange rate, the result flips. The rest of this guide explains why.

Three assumptions

  • Small open economy. The country is too small to affect world interest rates. It takes the world rate r* as given.
  • Perfect capital mobility. Investors move funds instantly to wherever returns are highest. If the domestic interest rate rose above r*, foreign money would pour in until it fell back. So the domestic rate is pinned: r = r*.
  • Fixed prices in the short run. The price level P does not change, so a change in the nominal exchange rate is also a change in the real exchange rate.

The second assumption does most of the work. In the closed-economy IS–LM model, the interest rate adjusts to clear the markets. Here it cannot move, so something else has to adjust: the exchange rate under a float, or the money supply under a peg.

The exchange rate

In this model e is the price of the domestic currency in foreign currency: how many units of foreign currency one unit of domestic currency buys.

  • A rise in e is an appreciation: the domestic currency gets stronger.
  • A fall in e is a depreciation: the domestic currency gets weaker.

When e rises, domestic goods become dearer for foreigners and foreign goods become cheaper at home. Exports fall and imports rise, so net exports fall. Net exports depend negatively on the exchange rate: NX = NX(e).

Reading Pakistani headlines

Newspapers quote the rupee as rupees per US dollar, for example Rs 280 per dollar. That is the inverse of e. If the rate moves from Rs 280 to Rs 300 per dollar, each rupee buys fewer dollars: the rupee has depreciated, and e in this model has fallen. A "stronger dollar" in the news means a lower e on the graph.

IS*: the goods market

IS*:  Y = C(Y − T) + I(r*) + G + NX(e)

Output equals planned spending: consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports. Because r is fixed at r*, investment is fixed too. The only price left to clear the goods market is the exchange rate.

Why IS* slopes downward

A higher exchange rate lowers net exports. Lower spending means lower output, magnified by the multiplier. So a high e goes with a low Y, and the curve slopes down in a graph with e on the vertical axis and Y on the horizontal.

This is the core link between exchange rates and output. In the simulator, a rise of 10 in e cuts net exports by 25. With a marginal propensity to consume of 0.75 the multiplier is 4, so the output the goods market can support falls by 100. Hover over the chart in the Lab to read this trade-off at any exchange rate.

What shifts IS*

Right: higher government spending, lower taxes, import restrictions, a lower world interest rate (which raises investment), or more confident households and firms. Left: the reverse.

LM*: the money market

LM*:  M/P = L(r*, Y)

Money demand depends on the interest rate and on income, not on the exchange rate. With r pinned at r* and the real money supply M/P given, only one level of income makes people willing to hold exactly that money. So LM* is a vertical line: it fixes output whatever the exchange rate.

What shifts LM*

Right: a larger money supply, a lower price level, or a higher r* (which lowers money demand, so the same money supports more income). Left: the reverse.

Equilibrium

The economy sits where IS* and LM* cross. LM* fixes output; IS* then tells you which exchange rate clears the goods market at that output.

Policy under floating rates

Under a floating rate the central bank chooses the money supply and the market sets the exchange rate.

Fiscal policy: no effect on output

Higher G shifts IS* to the right. LM* does not move, so output cannot change; the exchange rate rises instead. The chain runs: higher income would raise money demand → the domestic interest rate tends to rise above r* → foreign capital flows in → the currency appreciates → net exports fall by exactly the rise in G. The crowding out happens through the exchange rate, not through investment.

Monetary policy: strong effect

A larger money supply shifts LM* to the right. The domestic interest rate tends to fall below r*, capital flows out, the currency depreciates, net exports rise and output rises. Monetary policy works entirely through the exchange rate, because the interest rate cannot stay below r*.

Trade policy: no effect on output or the trade balance

Import quotas or tariffs shift IS* to the right, so e rises. The stronger currency cuts exports and raises other imports by just enough to cancel the quota. Output and net exports end up unchanged. Protection changes the mix of trade, not its balance.

Policy under fixed rates

How a peg works

The central bank announces a rate ē and stands ready to buy or sell domestic currency at that price. Traders then keep the market rate in line:

  • If the market rate drifts above ē, traders buy foreign currency cheaply in the market and sell it to the central bank for domestic currency. The money supply rises until the market rate is back at ē.
  • If the market rate drifts below ē, traders sell domestic currency to the central bank for foreign reserves. The money supply falls until the market rate is back at ē.

So under a peg, the money supply is no longer the central bank's choice. It is whatever amount holds e at ē. That is why the simulator moves the money-supply slider for you under a fixed rate.

Fiscal policy: strong effect

Higher G shifts IS* right and puts upward pressure on e. To defend the peg the central bank supplies domestic currency, so LM* shifts right too. Output rises by the full multiplier: ΔY = ΔG / (1 − MPC). In the simulator, a rise of 25 in G raises output by 100.

Monetary policy: no effect

A larger money supply shifts LM* right and puts downward pressure on e. Traders sell the extra domestic currency back to the central bank, and LM* returns to where it started. The only lasting effect is a loss of foreign reserves.

Trade policy: raises output and net exports

Import restrictions shift IS* right. The central bank expands M to hold the peg, so output rises, and because e cannot rise, net exports stay higher.

Devaluation

A pegging country can still change the peg. A devaluation (a lower ē) makes its goods cheaper abroad, raises net exports and lifts output, with the central bank supplying the money needed at the new rate. A revaluation does the reverse. In practice a devaluation also raises import prices and the burden of any debt owed in foreign currency, costs the model leaves out.

The impossible trinity

A country can have at most two of these three:

  • Free movement of capital
  • A fixed exchange rate
  • An independent monetary policy

The Mundell–Fleming results follow from it. With free capital flows, a country that fixes its exchange rate gives up monetary policy, as in the fixed-rate case above. A country that wants its own monetary policy with free capital flows must let its currency float. The only way to keep both a peg and an independent monetary policy is to restrict capital flows.

Standard examples: the United States lets the dollar float; Hong Kong ties its dollar to the US dollar and gives up independent monetary policy; China has for long periods managed its exchange rate while restricting capital flows.

Where the model falls short

  • Prices adjust over time. In the long run a monetary expansion under a float raises prices rather than output.
  • Capital is not perfectly mobile, and risk matters. Investors demand a risk premium on emerging-market assets, so domestic interest rates in countries such as Pakistan can sit well above world rates and move with confidence.
  • Most regimes sit in between. Many emerging economies run a managed float: the market sets the rate, but the central bank intervenes to smooth sharp moves. Pakistan describes its regime as market-based, while the State Bank of Pakistan can still step into the market.
  • Large economies are not price-takers. The United States or the euro area can move world interest rates.
  • Pegs can break. If markets doubt that the central bank has enough reserves, a speculative attack can force a devaluation.

Drawing it in an exam

  • Put the exchange rate e on the vertical axis and output Y on the horizontal axis.
  • Draw IS* sloping down and LM* vertical. Under a peg, add ē as a horizontal line.
  • Label shifted curves IS*1 → IS*2 and mark the old and new equilibria.
  • Write the mechanism as a chain: policy → curve shift → pressure on the interest rate → capital flows → e → NX → Y.
  • Under a peg, show LM* moving to meet IS* at ē, and say who moved it: the central bank defending the peg.

Check your understanding

1. Why is LM* vertical?

Money demand depends on r and Y, not on e. With r fixed at r* and M/P given, only one level of income clears the money market, whatever the exchange rate.

2. Under a floating rate the government cuts spending to reduce its budget deficit. What happens to Y, e and NX?

Output is unchanged, e falls (the currency depreciates) and net exports rise by the amount of the cut. A smaller budget deficit comes with a smaller trade deficit.

3. Under a peg, why can't the central bank print money to fight a recession?

Any rise in M puts downward pressure on e. To defend ē the central bank has to buy back the extra currency with its reserves, so M returns to where it was and output does not change.

4. A country with a peg is in recession and cannot raise spending. What can it do, and what does it cost?

It can devalue: a lower ē raises net exports and output. The costs are dearer imports, higher inflation, a heavier burden of foreign-currency debt and damage to the credibility of the peg.

5. The rupee moves from Rs 280 to Rs 300 per dollar. Did e rise or fall, and what should happen to net exports?

e fell: each rupee buys fewer dollars, so the rupee depreciated. Other things equal, net exports rise.

6. Why don't import quotas improve the trade balance under a floating rate?

They shift IS* right, but LM* fixes output, so e rises instead. The stronger currency cuts exports and raises other imports until net exports are back where they started.

The simulator's equations

Q is the import-restriction lever: it raises net exports by Q at any exchange rate. The multiplier is 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 4.

Floating rate: LM* gives Y = 2M + 40r*. IS* then gives the exchange rate, e = (A − 0.25Y) / 2.5, where A = 250 − 0.75T − 10r* + G + Q collects everything that does not depend on Y or e.

Fixed rate: IS* at e = ē gives Y = 4(A − 2.5ē), and the money market then fixes the money supply the peg requires, M = 0.5Y − 20r*.
