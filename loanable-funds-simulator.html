Every economy has people who earn more than they spend and people who want to spend more than they earn. The loanable funds model is about how the first group's saving reaches the second group's projects, and why government budgets change the price of that transfer: the real interest rate.

1The market for loanable funds

Banks, bond markets, stock markets and mutual funds all do the same basic job: they move money from savers to borrowers. The model treats all of them as a single market.

  • What is traded: funds that savers are willing to lend.
  • Who supplies them: anyone who saves. That means households, a government running a budget surplus, and foreign savers.
  • Who demands them: firms and home builders who borrow to buy new capital, which economists call investment.
  • The price: the real interest rate, r. It is the reward a lender receives and the cost a borrower pays, measured in goods rather than money. Roughly, r equals the nominal interest rate minus expected inflation.

This is a long-run model. Total output is fixed at its full-employment level (5,000 in the simulator). Fiscal policy therefore cannot change how much the economy produces. It changes how that output is divided between consumption, investment, government purchases and net exports, and the interest rate is the price that does the dividing.

2Where the funds come from

The supply side follows directly from national income accounting. In a closed economy, everything produced is either consumed, invested or bought by the government:

Y = C + I + G
Y − C − G = I   what is left after consumption and government purchases is investment
(Y − T − C) + (T − G) = I   add and subtract taxes T
Private saving + Public saving = National saving = Investment

So the pool of loanable funds has three possible sources. The simulator uses the same colours for each source on the graph, the flow diagram and the controls.

Households

Private saving = Y − T − C

Disposable income that is not spent on consumption. In real economies, firms' retained earnings count here too.

Government

Public saving = T − G

A budget surplus adds funds to the pool. A deficit is negative public saving: the government draws funds out.

Foreign savers

Net capital inflow

Foreigners buying more domestic assets than residents buy abroad. It is zero in a closed economy.

Why the supply curve slopes upward

A higher real interest rate raises the reward for waiting. A dollar saved today buys (1 + r) dollars of goods next year, so when r rises, consuming now becomes more expensive relative to consuming later. Households consume a little less and save a little more. In the simulator, private saving rises by 20 for every one-point rise in r; you can change this with the saving sensitivity slider. When capital can cross borders, a higher domestic rate also attracts foreign funds, which makes the supply curve flatter.

What shifts supply

Anything that changes saving at a given interest rate shifts the curve: the government's budget balance, household thrift, expectations about future taxes, the world interest rate and how freely capital moves.

Taxes work on both kinds of saving. When net taxes rise by 100, public saving rises by 100. But households pay the tax partly by consuming less (MPC × 100 = 75) and partly by saving less (25). National saving rises by only 75. The same logic in reverse explains why a tax cut moves the interest rate less than an equal increase in spending.

3Who borrows the funds

The demand for loanable funds comes from investment: spending on new capital goods such as machines, factories, software, new housing and additions to inventories. Buying existing shares or bonds is not investment in this sense. It is saving changing hands.

The decision rule

A firm goes ahead with a project when its expected real return is higher than the real interest rate. This holds even when the firm pays with its own cash, because the interest it could have earned by lending that cash is the opportunity cost.

Why the demand curve slopes downward

Imagine every possible project in the economy ranked from highest return to lowest. At a high interest rate only the best projects pass the test. As r falls, more projects pass, so the quantity of funds demanded rises. In the simulator, investment falls by 40 for every one-point rise in r (the investment sensitivity slider).

What shifts demand

  • Expected profitability and business confidence
  • New technologies that create profitable projects
  • Corporate tax rates and investment tax credits
  • The price of capital goods and how much capital already exists

Keep the difference between a shift and a movement clear. If a budget deficit pushes r up, investment falls along an unchanged demand curve. Firms still have the same projects; fewer of them clear the higher bar.

Crowding out

When the government borrows more, the interest rate rises until somebody else gives up the funds. Firms cancel marginal projects, so investment falls. Households trim consumption, so private saving rises. If capital is mobile, foreign savers lend more. The part that comes out of investment is called crowding out. For a shift in public saving, the share is:

crowding-out share = d ÷ (h + d + k)
d = investment sensitivity, h = saving sensitivity, k = capital mobility
With the current settings: 40 ÷ 60 = 67%

Why a tax cut moves r less than a spending increase

A 200 increase in government purchases removes 200 from national saving. A 200 tax cut also adds 200 to the deficit, but it puts 200 into households' pockets, and they save a quarter of it when the MPC is 0.75. That private saving partly refills the pool, so supply shifts by only 150.

Crowding in

Run the film backwards. Cutting a deficit frees funds, lowers the interest rate and raises investment. This is the core long-run argument for fiscal discipline: a smaller deficit means more of today's output goes into capital that raises future output.

About the investment tax credit. A credit shifts investment demand to the right, so it raises r, and part of its effect is lost to the higher cost of borrowing. In reality a credit also costs tax revenue; the simulator treats it as revenue-neutral so you can see the demand effect on its own.

5Two ways to draw a deficit

Textbooks draw a budget deficit in one of two ways, and the simulator can show both.

  • Deficit lowers supply. Supply is national saving, so a deficit (negative public saving) shifts the supply curve to the left. Demand is investment only.
  • Deficit adds to demand. Supply is private saving, plus any surplus and capital inflow. The government's borrowing joins investment on the demand side, so a deficit shifts demand to the right.

Both pictures rest on the same equation, rearranged:

Private saving + (T − G) + Capital inflow = Investment
Private saving + Capital inflow = Investment + (G − T)

So the equilibrium interest rate is identical. Only the quantity on the horizontal axis differs: in the first view it measures funds going to investment, in the second it measures all funds borrowed, including by the government.

6When crowding out is weaker

How sensitive saving and investment are to r

If investment barely responds to the interest rate (a steep demand curve), deficits raise r but crowd out little investment. If saving responds strongly (a flat supply curve), most of the government's borrowing is funded by households consuming less. The crowding-out share d ÷ (h + d + k) captures both.

Ricardian equivalence

Robert Barro argued in 1974 that forward-looking households might see through government borrowing. If they expect today's debt to be repaid with future taxes, a debt-financed tax cut leaves their lifetime wealth unchanged, so they save all of it. Private saving rises exactly as public saving falls, and the interest rate does not move. The simulator's Ricardian foresight slider sets the share of the deficit households treat as future taxes.

Few economists think the offset is complete. Households that cannot borrow, have short planning horizons, or expect someone else to pay future taxes will spend part of a tax cut. Most studies find partial offsets.

Open economies and twin deficits

When capital moves freely, a domestic interest rate above the world rate pulls in foreign funds. In a small open economy with perfect capital mobility, r is pinned to the world rate: a deficit is financed from abroad and domestic investment is barely touched.

The inflow has a counterpart. In the national accounts, net exports equal minus the net capital inflow, so a country that borrows from abroad must be importing more than it exports. A budget deficit financed this way arrives with a trade deficit, the "twin deficits" often discussed for the United States in the 1980s. Large economies cannot fully escape: their borrowing can push up the world rate itself.

What the model leaves out

The loanable funds model holds output at full employment. In a recession with idle workers and machines, extra government spending can raise income, and higher income raises saving, so crowding out can be much smaller. Central banks also set short-term rates directly, and lenders may demand higher rates when debt looks risky. For short-run questions, pair this model with IS-LM or AS-AD.

7The simulator's equations

All quantities are in $ billions per year, and r is in percent. The model is calibrated so that the baseline economy has output 5,000, consumption 3,100, investment 900, government purchases and net taxes of 1,000, and r = 5%.

Private saving: Sp = Y − T − C
Consumption: C = 3,100 + MPC × [(Y − T̃) − 4,000] − h(r − 5) − thrift
Taxes households expect: T̃ = T + θ(G − T)  θ = Ricardian foresight
Public saving: Sg = T − G
Net capital inflow: KI = k(r − rworld)
Investment: I = 900 + tax credit + profitability − d(r − 5)
Equilibrium: Sp + Sg + KI = I

Solving the equilibrium condition gives the interest rate directly:

r = 5 + [I(5) − Sp(5) − Sg − k(5 − rworld)] ÷ (h + d + k)
With the current settings:
r = 5 + [900 − 900 − 0 − 0] ÷ 60 = 5.00%

8Key terms

Real interest rate
The interest rate adjusted for inflation; the price of loanable funds.
National saving
Output not used for consumption or government purchases: private saving plus public saving.
Public saving
Net taxes minus government purchases. Positive with a surplus, negative with a deficit.
Net capital inflow
Foreign purchases of domestic assets minus domestic purchases of foreign assets. Equal to minus net exports.
Investment
Spending on new capital goods, new housing and inventories.
Crowding out
The fall in private investment caused by higher interest rates when the government borrows more.
Crowding in
The rise in private investment when lower government borrowing reduces interest rates.
Ricardian equivalence
The idea that households save a debt-financed tax cut in anticipation of future taxes, leaving national saving unchanged.
Twin deficits
A budget deficit financed by foreign borrowing, matched by a trade deficit.
