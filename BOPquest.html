    The balance of payments as a national ledger

    What the BoP records, who counts as a resident, and the three accounts.

    The balance of payments records every economic transaction between the residents of Pakistan and the rest of the world over a period: a month, a quarter or a fiscal year (Pakistan's runs from July to June). Residents are the households, firms, government and central bank whose main economic interest lies in Pakistan.

    Residence depends on where someone lives and works, whatever their nationality. A Pakistani nurse who has worked in Riyadh for years is a resident of Saudi Arabia, so the money she sends to her family in Pakistan crosses a border and enters the BoP.

    Two sides of every page

    A shopkeeper's bahi khata has a jama column for money received and a naam column for money paid out. The BoP works the same way. Credit (jama) entries bring foreign currency in: exports, remittances, foreign investment and loans received. Debit (naam) entries send it out: imports, interest and profits paid abroad, and loan repayments.

    Three accounts

    • The current account covers day-to-day earning and spending: goods, services, income and transfers.
    • The capital account covers capital transfers, such as debt forgiveness, and a few non-financial assets. For Pakistan it is tiny.
    • The financial account covers investment and borrowing: what foreigners invest in or lend to Pakistan, what Pakistanis invest abroad, and the State Bank's reserves.

    A fourth line, net errors and omissions, absorbs the statistical gaps that remain because no country records every transaction perfectly.

    Who publishes it

    The State Bank of Pakistan compiles the BoP every month using the IMF's sixth manual (BPM6) and releases it in the middle of the following month. The figures for the latest months are provisional and are often revised.

    Why it matters

    Pakistan has faced repeated balance-of-payments crises. When dollars going out persistently exceed dollars coming in, reserves fall, the rupee weakens and the country turns to the IMF.

    The current account

    Goods, services, primary income and secondary income, with Pakistan's numbers.

    The current account is Pakistan's day-to-day external earning and spending. It has four parts.

    Goods

    Physical products crossing the border. Pakistan sells textiles, rice, leather goods and surgical instruments; it buys crude oil and fuel, LNG, machinery, vehicles, palm oil and chemicals. In FY26 goods imports of $64.5bn were more than double goods exports of $30.8bn, a deficit of $33.7bn.

    Services

    Work done for or by foreigners: freight and transport, travel (including Hajj and Umrah), telecoms and computer services, insurance and government services. Services exports rose to $10.0bn in FY26 from $8.5bn in FY25, which cut the services deficit to $1.9bn.

    Primary income

    Payments for the use of capital and labour: interest on foreign loans, profits and dividends of foreign-owned firms, and wages of people working abroad for less than a year. Pakistan received $1.2bn and paid $9.7bn in FY26. This $8.5bn deficit is the running cost of past borrowing and foreign investment.

    Secondary income

    One-way transfers with nothing given in return. Workers' remittances dominate: a record $41.6bn in FY26, more than all goods exports. With other transfers, the surplus reached $43.7bn.

    Trade balance or current account?

    News reports often quote the trade deficit from Pakistan Bureau of Statistics customs data. The SBP's BoP records payments, so its goods figures differ: PBS put FY26 imports at $69.8bn, SBP at $64.5bn.

    Who sends the remittances

    Saudi Arabia and the UAE are the two largest sources, followed by the UK and the US. In September 2026 Saudi Arabia alone sent $899m.

    The capital and financial accounts

    How a current account gap gets paid for.

    When the current account is in deficit, Pakistan spends more foreign currency than it earns. The gap is covered by investment and borrowing from abroad, or by drawing down the State Bank's reserves.

    Capital account

    Small for Pakistan: $0.13bn in FY26. It records capital transfers, such as a creditor forgiving a loan or a donor paying to rebuild flood-damaged bridges, and purchases of non-produced assets, such as land bought for an embassy.

    Financial account

    This account records changes in Pakistan's foreign assets and liabilities. A net inflow means foreigners added more claims on Pakistan (investment and loans) than Pakistanis added abroad.

    • Direct investment: a lasting stake of 10% or more in a firm, such as a foreign company building a plant or buying a local business. Net inflow in FY26: $1.45bn, about 0.3% of GDP.
    • Portfolio investment: shares and bonds below the 10% threshold, including the government's Eurobonds and Sukuk. FY26 saw a net outflow of $1.18bn: redemptions and sales by foreign holders exceeded new issues.
    • Other investment: loans from the World Bank, ADB, China and commercial banks, deposits placed by friendly countries, rollovers and trade credit. Net inflow in FY26: $1.77bn.
    • Reserve assets: the State Bank's foreign currency, gold and SDRs. They rose by $3.66bn in FY26.

    Where the IMF goes

    In the SBP's table, IMF lending appears as use of Fund credit and loans, inside a block called reserves and related items, because it goes straight to the State Bank's reserves. Net IMF lending was $1.82bn in FY26.

    The Eurobond quarter

    On 3 September 2026 Pakistan sold $3bn of Eurobonds in two tranches, its largest international bond issue in a single deal, against about $6bn of orders. That one deal explains most of the $2.94bn portfolio inflow in July–September 2026.

    Other recent borrowing

    A $750m three-year Eurobond in April 2026 and Pakistan's first Panda bond, sold in China's market, in May 2026.

    The balancing identity

    Double entry, the overall balance, and how to read the SBP's signs.

    The BoP is a double-entry system: every transaction is recorded twice, once as a credit and once as a debit. Suppose a towel mill in Faisalabad ships $1m of towels to a buyer in Spain.

    • Entry one: +$1m goods exports, in the current account.
    • Entry two: the buyer pays into the mill's dollar account, so the bank's claims on the rest of the world rise by $1m. That increase in Pakistan's foreign assets is recorded in the financial account.

    Because each transaction has a matching entry, the accounts must add up. The State Bank presents the result like this:

    Current account + Capital account + Net financial inflows + Errors and omissions = Overall balance

    The overall balance, plus any IMF lending, equals the change in the State Bank's reserves. A deficit has to be met from reserves or from the IMF; a surplus adds to reserves.

    Reading the SBP's signs

    The SBP follows BPM6, which measures the financial account as assets acquired minus liabilities incurred. So when foreigners invest in or lend to Pakistan, the number is negative. FY26's −2,029 means a net inflow of $2.03bn.

    The table's Overall Balance line uses the same convention: a minus sign there means reserves went up. The next line, Reserves and Related Items, repeats the figure with a plus sign.

    A history of boom and bust

    Pakistan's current account has swung hard. Fast growth pulls in imports, the deficit balloons, reserves drain and the country turns to the IMF. That happened in FY18, when the deficit hit a record $19.2bn, and again in FY22 ($17.5bn) as oil prices spiked. By 3 February 2023 the State Bank's reserves had fallen to $2.92bn.

    Import controls, a weaker rupee, high interest rates and IMF support then compressed the deficit. In FY25 the current account showed a surplus of $1.84bn, its first annual surplus in over a decade.

    The IMF programme

    A 37-month, $7bn Extended Fund Facility was approved on 25 September 2024. A $1.4bn climate facility (RSF) was added in May 2025. The third review released about $1.3bn in May 2026. A staff-level agreement on the fourth review, reached in October 2026, would release about $1.21bn more once the IMF Board approves it, taking total disbursements to about $5.7bn.

    FY26: near balance, held up by remittances

    In FY26 goods imports grew 9% while goods exports fell 5%, so the goods deficit widened by $6.9bn to $33.7bn. Record remittances and stronger services exports absorbed almost all of it, leaving a deficit of $0.39bn.

    FY27 so far: imports running ahead

    In July–September 2026 goods imports rose 14.3% and exports 7.7%, so the current account deficit reached $0.87bn in one quarter. Remittances (up 14% to $10.9bn) and services exports (up 23%) cushioned the gap. Topline Securities expects a deficit of $2–3bn for the full year, 0.4–0.6% of GDP.

    What to watch

    Oil. Petroleum imports were $16.9bn in FY26 (PBS), about a quarter of the import bill. The SBP raised rates in October 2026, citing the Middle East conflict keeping energy prices high.

    Imports as growth returns. Each recovery so far has widened the deficit.

    Interest and profits. Payments abroad cost about $9.7bn a year.

    FDI. At $1.45bn net it covers little of the financing need, so much of it is borrowed.

    Talking points for class

    • Remittances exceeded goods exports in FY26. Is a current account balanced by remittances as safe as one balanced by exports?
    • Reserves rose to $21.6bn by October 2026 partly through Eurobond borrowing. How should we judge reserves built with borrowed money?
    • Import restrictions closed the deficit in FY23 but cut growth. Why do they tend to fail as a long-run fix?
    • Primary income outflows of about $8.5bn a year are the cost of past borrowing. What would reduce them?
